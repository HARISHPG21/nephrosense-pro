📖 Plain-Language Guide

What is Kidney Disease?

Everything you need to know about chronic kidney disease — explained simply, without medical jargon.

🫘

What are kidneys?

Two bean-shaped organs that clean your blood, remove waste through urine, and control fluid balance in your body.

🚨

What is CKD?

When kidneys slowly lose their ability to clean blood properly. It's called "chronic" because it develops over months or years — often silently.

😶

Why is it silent?

Kidneys can work with just 15% function without showing obvious symptoms. Most people only discover CKD during a routine blood test.

The 5 Stages of Kidney Disease — From Mild to Severe

The eGFR score (kidney filtering score) determines which stage you're in. Higher = healthier.

Stage 1 — Mild

eGFR ≥ 90 (Normal)

Kidneys work normally but some signs of damage exist (like protein in urine). Monitor once a year.

Stage 2 — Mild

eGFR 60–89

Slightly reduced but still working well. No symptoms usually. Regular check-ups needed.

Stage 3 — Moderate

eGFR 30–59

Noticeable reduction. You may feel tired or have swelling. See a kidney specialist.

Stage 4 — Severe

eGFR 15–29

Kidneys struggling badly. Prepare for possible dialysis or transplant soon.

Stage 5 — Failure

eGFR < 15

Kidneys have almost stopped. Dialysis or kidney transplant is urgently required.

Understanding Your Test Results

What each number on your report actually means

Creatinine is a waste product created when your muscles work. Healthy kidneys filter it out of your blood into urine. If creatinine builds up in your blood, it means your kidneys aren't filtering properly. Normal: 0.6–1.2 mg/dL for men, 0.5–1.1 for women. Even a small rise above normal deserves attention.
eGFR stands for "estimated Glomerular Filtration Rate" — essentially, how well your kidneys are filtering your blood per minute. Think of it like a car engine efficiency rating. Above 90 is normal. Below 60 for 3+ months means kidney disease. Doctors use eGFR to decide treatments and drug dosages.
Blood Urea Nitrogen (BUN) is another waste product — this one comes from breaking down proteins you eat. Healthy kidneys remove it. High BUN usually means kidneys are struggling, but it can also go up from dehydration or eating a lot of protein. Normal: 7–25 mg/dL. Doctors often look at both BUN and creatinine together.
Your kidneys produce a hormone called EPO that tells your bone marrow to make red blood cells. When kidneys are damaged, they produce less EPO → fewer red blood cells → anemia (low blood). This is why people with kidney disease often feel tired and weak. Normal hemoglobin: 12+ g/dL for women, 13+ for men.
Healthy kidneys act like a fine filter — they keep protein inside your blood and only let waste out into urine. When kidneys are damaged, the filter develops "holes" and protein leaks into urine. Finding protein in urine is one of the earliest warning signs of kidney damage, often appearing before any symptoms.

Who Gets Kidney Disease & How to Prevent It

Risk factors, warning signs, and what you can do

The biggest risk factors are: Diabetes (causes 40% of all kidney disease), High blood pressure (second most common cause), being over 60, having a family member with kidney disease, being overweight, and taking certain pain medicines (like ibuprofen/Brufen) regularly for years.
Things you can change: Control your blood sugar and blood pressure, maintain healthy weight, quit smoking, stay hydrated, avoid overusing painkillers, exercise regularly. Things you can't change: Age, family history, genetic conditions. Focus energy on what you can control — it makes a real difference!
Most people have NO symptoms early on — that's why regular tests matter. When symptoms do appear: foamy or bubbly urine (protein leaking), swollen ankles or feet, going to toilet more often at night, feeling very tired for no reason, loss of appetite, difficulty concentrating, or itchy skin. See a doctor if you notice these.
See a kidney specialist if: your eGFR drops below 30, your creatinine is rising over time, you have a lot of protein in urine, your blood pressure is very hard to control, or you have blood in your urine along with protein. Early specialist care reduces hospitalizations and slows disease progression significantly.
Drink enough water (6–8 glasses/day), eat less salt (less than one teaspoon/day), limit red meat, avoid processed foods, maintain a healthy weight, exercise 30 minutes most days, and don't smoke. If you have diabetes, keeping blood sugar controlled is the single most important thing you can do to protect your kidneys.

Official Project Title
A Machine Learning–Based System for Early Prediction of Chronic Kidney Disease

NephroSense Pro is a machine learning research project developed by P.G. Harish (Team Leader), Nirmal, and Vishnu Vardhan, demonstrating ensemble classification for early CKD detection.

The system is trained and validated on the UCI CKD Dataset (400 instances, 24 features) with 10-fold stratified cross-validation and GridSearchCV hyperparameter optimization. SHAP integration ensures predictions are not just accurate but clinically explainable.

The best ensemble model achieves 98.8% accuracy, 0.993 AUC-ROC, and near-zero false positive rate on the held-out test set.
